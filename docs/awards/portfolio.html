Engineering Portfolio¶

What Is an Engineering Portfolio?¶

The Engineering Portfolio is a document of up to 15 pages, printed or digital, that tells the story of a team’s season. It is not an archive of everything the team did. It is a curated story of how the team thinks, works, and grows.

Archive

Story

Content

Everything, useful or not

Selected for the award criteria

Order

By date, mixed together

Grouped by award or by topic

Analysis

None; the judge has to dig for it

Causes, results, and conclusions are shown

While it is tempting to include every fun moment of the season, judges evaluate teams against specific award criteria. Sort your material by how well it answers a criterion, not by how interesting it is. The more filler a page has, the more likely the information a judge needs goes unnoticed.

Tip

  • Put yourself in the judge’s place. Imagine you have three minutes to find everything about one award in someone else’s portfolio. If you can’t, neither can they.

  • Call things by their names. A clear, structured page beats a beautiful but vague one.

How Judges Use the Portfolio¶

The portfolio is one of several sources judges use. Their decision combines:

  • The portfolio: the written document, up to 15 pages.

  • The pit interview: the team presents and answers questions.

  • Observation: how the team behaves at the event, in the pits and on the field.

  • Judges’ deliberation: judges are often assigned to a single award category.

The portfolio is not fully self-contained, because the interview adds to it. However, there are moments when judges see only the portfolio, so a thought should never be cut off halfway.

Because judges are usually assigned to one award, they need to find “their” content fast. A Connect judge may love your odometry algorithm, but they will score you on Connect. This is why it is convenient to organize the portfolio by award.

For awards where it is not required, the portfolio still works for the team. Judges use it as evidence of the work done and to look information up quickly. A photo, a result, or a statistic on the page adds weight to everything you say in the interview.

New teams should not try to cover every award at once. Start with the awards where the portfolio is required, then grow toward covering the criteria of every award.

What Judges Do Not Do¶

  • They don’t open links. Websites, videos, code repositories, and social media in the portfolio are not viewed. Don’t spend space on them.

  • They don’t accept additional materials for deliberation. Other printed or digital materials are not part of the evaluation.

  • They don’t score the cover. The cover page exists to identify the team, not to meet criteria.

  • They don’t read past page 15. Anything beyond 15 pages is not considered, even if you hand in 68 pages.

  • They don’t score design. Judges evaluate content, not color schemes or decorative frames. The portfolio is not a glossy magazine; it must be structured and easy to read.

Everything that should influence the decision must be inside the first 15 pages.

Tip

  • Additional materials can still help in the interview. For example, a team with complex algorithm math brought a separate printout of the calculations. Judges looked at it in the pits as a visual aid, but did not take it or score it.

  • A page after page 15 (such as a back cover with social media QR codes) is fine for outreach events, as long as nothing judges need is on it.

Formal Requirements¶

The exact rules are in the Competition Manual for your season; always check the current version. The key numbers:

Requirement

Value

Cover page

1 page; the team number is required on it

Content pages

15 maximum (8 sheets printed double-sided, plus the cover)

Paper size

A4 or US Letter (8.5” × 11”), portrait or landscape

Minimum font size

10 pt

Digital file size

Under 15 MB

Eligible work

Only work done after January 1 of the year the season’s game is released

A few notes on these rules:

  • Count pages, not sheets. Each printed side counts as one page. Don’t try to sneak in a 16th page; judges will notice.

  • Keep the table of contents current. An outdated table of contents looks careless and frustrates judges. Update page numbers every time you rearrange the portfolio.

  • Don’t go below 10 pt. Smaller text becomes unreadable on paper.

  • Check the file size early. Many events now ask for a digital copy in advance so several judges can read in parallel. A file that is too large may be rejected.

  • Off-season work counts if it is for this season. The January 1 date matters most for outreach. Technical work done in spring or summer to prepare for the new season can be included.

Challenges Yes, Hardship Stories No¶

The Competition Manual asks teams not to include stories of hardship. Awards are not given for having a harder time than other teams. The winner is an example for others, not a victim of circumstances.

This does not mean hiding failures. A portfolio where everything worked the first time and the team never asked for help looks artificial, and judges will sense it. Keep the balance:

Show

Don’t write

The difficulties you faced and how you overcame them

Pages of complaints about how bad things were

Persistence, determination, problem-solving

Requests for sympathy

Analysis and how you applied what you learned

Struggles with no conclusion

Throughout the portfolio, check that the tone stays consistent with FIRST Core Values and Gracious Professionalism®. Slang and emotional asides can read as ambiguous to a judge.

Collecting Material During the Season¶

The portfolio is built over the whole season, not in the week before an event. Most teams have scrambled at the last minute at least once; setting up a process now prevents it.

What to Document¶

  • Photograph whiteboards. Brainstorms and calculations get erased. Judges want to see how calculations shaped the design. Make it a habit to photograph any physical note and drop it in the team chat.

  • Photograph meetings and events. Digital photos carry a date and time, so they help rebuild the season’s timeline. Even an unused photo reminds you that an event happened.

  • Keep an engineering notebook. Members write down ideas, gear ratios, autonomous pseudocode, calculations, and strategy. A weekly entry is enough. Most of it won’t reach the portfolio, but forgotten work can’t be used at all.

  • Count outreach in numbers. Judges have no time to read everything. Write down the key numbers and a few events, and be ready to tell the rest in the interview.

Important

Quality over quantity. One event that reached 10,000 people says more than a hundred events that reached ten each. Don’t chase numbers, but record them when you have them.

Post-Event Interviews¶

After each competition, ask every member to document their area (software, a mechanism, outreach) while memories are fresh and the current robot version is still in hand to photograph or screenshot in CAD. Text or a voice message both work. Less experienced members can answer five questions:

  1. What were the problems?

  2. What did we try?

  3. What worked, and what didn’t?

  4. Why did we choose this solution?

  5. What would we do differently now?

The answers are ready-made material for the problem → solution → result chain. Use the time between events to turn them into portfolio pages.

Store Everything, and Name It Clearly¶

  • Proof. If you started a team, mentor one, or donated equipment, ask for a signed thank-you letter. At a fair, take a certificate of participation.

  • Clear file names. Use Portfolio_2026-11-27.pdf, not fdhg_47676385.pdf. A date in the name prevents losing versions.

  • Failed versions. Keep old versions of mechanisms and code, and photograph prototypes that didn’t work. Without them you can’t show progress or lessons learned.

It is better to have too much material and filter it than to find out you have nothing.

Structuring the Portfolio¶

Two Ways to Organize¶

There are two common ways to organize a portfolio:

  • By award. Each award gets its own pages: for example, two pages for Think, then two for Control, and so on. This is the recommended approach for new teams. It helps students learn which criteria belong to which award and makes each award easy for its judge to find.

  • By area. Content is grouped into four areas of team life, each covering several awards. This is harder to keep consistent, but reads as one story.

Area

What it covers

Team

Role assignment, passing on experience, collaboration within the team

Engineering

Prototypes and versions, calculations, strategy, mechanism descriptions

Software

Algorithms, sensors, paths, control, automation

Outreach

Planning, finances, events, skills, social media, other teams

Choose Your Focus Awards¶

The advancing awards fall into three groups:

Group

Awards

MCI (Machine, Creativity, and Innovation)

Innovate, Design, Control

TA (Team Attributes)

Connect, Reach, Sustain

Think

Think

To be considered for Inspire, a team must be a strong contender for at least one award in each group. You don’t need to cover every award at once. Pick one focus award per group (for example Control, Sustain, and Think), then add others as the team grows.

Criteria Are a Compass, Not a Checklist¶

Criteria help the team decide where to grow this season, find its strengths and gaps, and plan work and roles. The engineering criteria point at planning, analysis, calculations, and conclusions; following them makes the work itself better.

Warning

Never do something only to satisfy a criterion. Judges evaluate the team’s real journey. A formula pasted in from Wikipedia after the fact is obvious, and students can’t explain work they didn’t do, either in the portfolio or in the interview. The same goes for outreach: an event held just to tick a box is easy to spot. If a criterion isn’t covered, that’s not the end of the world.

15 Pages Is a Maximum, Not a Target¶

At the start of the season, decide how many pages each area or award gets. A budget keeps the portfolio from sprawling and forces you to filter. Expect to rebalance later.

Workflow: Template → Plan → Iterations¶

  1. Week 1: design template. Set fonts, highlight styles, frames, and page elements for all pages, so layout work goes fast later.

  2. Weeks 2–3: page plan. For each page, decide the images, the text, and the target award or key criterion.

  3. Then: layout → review → edits, 2–3 rounds. Each member lays out their pages, then the team meets and discusses improvements. Pages get shorter and more connected with each round.

Leave plenty of time for edits, or nonsense and typos will slip through. Some teams have a media group that does the layout while each subteam supplies content. Either way, the person who did the work should write the first draft of that section. They know it best, and they will know what the portfolio says when judges ask.

Writing the Content¶

Show the Chain, Not Just the Facts¶

The robot standing next to you shows the result. The portfolio is where you show the journey that led to it. Judges care more about how you got there than about where you ended up.

  1. Problem

  2. Ideas

  3. Solution

  4. Prototype

  5. Testing

  6. Failure

  7. Improvement (back to testing until it works)

  8. Result

Be Honest¶

Don’t be afraid to say something didn’t work: “We tried X. It didn’t work, and here is why. So we changed Y. After that, we got Z.” Describe the path you actually took, not the one you planned or wished for. Embellishment is easy to spot and won’t help you.

Meaningful Headings and Keywords¶

Headings. A judge should know what a page is about from its heading alone. Avoid team slang and vague titles, and keep the content under each heading on that topic.

No

Yes

Keywords. Each award has marker words in its criteria. If you are going for an award, highlight them, sparingly. A judge should be able to tell which criterion a paragraph addresses at a glance:

We performed a trade-off analysis of three claw options and recorded the lessons learned for the next version.

Layout and Submission¶

Template First, Then Pages¶

Design one test page and lock in:

  • The background (dark text on a white background reads best)

  • Fonts for headings (20–30 pt), subheadings, body text (at least 10 pt), and captions

  • Highlight colors, each with its own purpose

  • Recurring elements: page number, photo caption, table style, keyword highlight

One Visual Anchor per Page¶

The anchor is the first thing a judge’s eye lands on: a big number, a photo, a diagram, a boxed one-line conclusion, or a large heading. Two anchors compete for attention; with none, the page blends into gray text.

Photos and CAD Must Explain¶

Photos

CAD and drawings

Have a caption and a clear link to the text block

Show what the text is about

Use arrows to point at what matters

Show the mechanism together with the game element

Match the real robot

Hand sketches are fine too

Even with the robot in front of them, judges can’t see every hidden mechanism. Terms like “tilter” or “linkage” aren’t slang, but a picture makes them instantly clear.

Small Fixes That Save a Page¶

  • Letter spacing. If a word doesn’t fit in a cell, reduce spacing slightly while keeping it readable.

  • Two columns. Split a large text block in half, left and right, and break it into paragraphs.

  • Scale check. Not sure a small symbol is visible? Print the page on plain A4 paper, or view it at a similar size on screen.

  • Charts from data. Graphs and diagrams prove an engineering approach. Google Sheets, Excel, or Matplotlib all work.

The Cover Page¶

The team number is required. Without it, judges will not review the portfolio. Recommended additions:

  • Team name and motto

  • The final robot: a photo or CAD render

  • Table of contents: which award or topic is on which page

  • Social media QR codes for outreach guests (not for judges) can go on the back cover, as page 16

  • Facts are checked

  • Results are shown

  • Process is shown

  • Conclusions are drawn

  • No unverified numbers

Layout

  • Consistent fonts

  • Consistent margins and spacing

  • Reads quickly

  • No overloaded pages

  • Good-quality photos

  • Team number on the cover

Printing Is Part of the Plan¶

Print at least a day before the event, not on the morning of competition. Find out in advance where you will print, run a test print, and ask other teams; some may offer to print for you.

  • Ink. A dark background looks great but is expensive to print. Keep only the cover dark and use white inside.

  • Borderless printing. Edge-to-edge designs need a larger sheet. Print on a bigger sheet and trim with a paper cutter.

  • Binding. For qualifiers, a binder or folder with a clear cover works. For regionals and championships, use professional printing. Plan margins for the binding.

  • Digital submission. Keep the file under 15 MB. The event director will say when and how to submit.

Preparing for the Judging Interview¶

The portfolio and the interview work together: the portfolio is the written record, the interview is where the team brings it to life.

How the Interview Works¶

Stage

What happens

First stage (once)

A prepared team presentation (5 minutes), then judges’ questions (10–15 minutes)

Second stage (several judge panels)

Questions tied to specific awards

Good and Poor Preparation¶

Well prepared

Poorly prepared

Understand what exactly is being asked

Memorized the text and the answers

Support the story with visuals

Get lost when a question is phrased differently

Answer briefly and to the point

Don’t know what to do after forgetting part of an answer

Give a concrete example from their own work

Try to say everything they know

Speak with energy and interest

Sound flat, as if forced to be there

A good interview is a lively conversation between people who love what they do. Preparation exists to reduce stress, build confidence, and free up time for content. The team should know in advance what will happen, who speaks about what, which topics each member knows well, and what to do with an unexpected question.

When to Start¶

A new team should not start drilling interviews on day one. From the start of the season, focus on the robot and outreach, accumulating material, decisions, versions, mistakes, and projects. Around December, start active preparation: turn it all into a presentation and practice answering.

Writing and Splitting the Speech¶

Condense the team’s whole season into 5 minutes. Try to cover as many criteria as possible, refer to concrete examples, include key results, and use the judges’ marker words.

Don’t split it as “you take slide 1, I take slide 2.” Decide instead:

  • Who is responsible for which area

  • Who can answer which potential questions

  • Who stands where

  • Who shows what

  • Who holds the poster or extra materials

  • Who advances the slides

Visuals Help You Answer¶

Judges absorb information better when they can see it, not just hear it.

Visual

Use it to show

The robot

A specific mechanism

Poster or diagram

A process or an architecture

Presentation

A chart or results

Extra materials

Prototypes, CAD, test photos

Rehearse in Stages¶

  1. Just say the speech.

  2. Fit it into the time limit.

  3. Add the robot, posters, or slides.

  4. Add questions.

  5. Rehearse with unfamiliar adults.

  6. Recreate competition conditions as closely as possible.

Rehearse behavior, not only content: no one laughs, no one interrupts, no one forgets their part.

Tip

Rehearse with strangers. A coach knows the team, its story, and what a student meant to say. A stranger doesn’t. They will phrase questions differently, use unfamiliar words, and ask about things the team considered obvious, just like a real judge.

Answering Questions¶

A perfect 5-minute speech is not an interview. The hardest part is not inventing a perfect answer, but understanding what answer is actually wanted. Work through four steps:

  1. What am I being asked?

  2. What information do they want?

  3. What from our experience proves it?

  4. How can I answer as briefly as possible?

Question

Weak answer

Strong answer

“What skills did you gain from working on the team?”

“We learned to program, and we also have a robot…“

Name the skills, list them, back each with an example

“Why did you choose this design?”

Everything about the design

Problem → chosen solution → result

Aim for thought + explanation + specifics:

Too little

Enough

Too much

“Because it’s more convenient.”

“We chose this design because we needed to reduce weight and free up space for mechanism X. After testing two options, we kept this one because it was 200 g lighter.”

Three minutes on the robot’s history, every design variant, and the time someone forgot to charge the battery

General questions about the team, robot, engineering decisions, programming, teamwork, outreach, and skills can be found in public question banks. Individual questions can’t be fully prepared for: they come from what your team says. Your speech raises questions, and those become your individual questions.
